# TAL product and positioning

## What TAL is

**AI can only run what’s written down.** Most of how a company actually works
never was; that gap is where AI pilots stall. **TAL makes organizations explicit**: from the authority at the top to
the code underneath.

TAL is a company that consults and builds products. The products and the
consulting are one body of work: the products are the problems met in every
organization, solved once and given away; the engagements apply the same
thinking inside one organization.

## The buyer

Operations, technology, and transformation leaders in brownfield, often
regulated enterprises whose AI pilots underperformed and whose postmortems
blamed the model. The real cause: the organization was only half written down,
and agents can only run the half that is.

## The page

The page is **TAL's positions**: what it holds to be true about organizations
now. It is not a portfolio, a services menu, or a résumé.

1. **Cover**: the point of view, stated as fact.
2. **Positions**: each one a claim, a drawn proof, and at most one line for
   what the proof cannot show.
3. **What We Do**: the cycle each value stream goes through, demonstrated.
4. **Work**: the book, ONTOK, and TCA, as evidence that the positions are
   practiced. Each stands on its own.
5. **Founder**: who he is, declared, and the door: one action and the
   address.

### Current positions

- Every company runs twice: on paper, and in practice.
- Authority no one wrote down can’t be handed to an agent.
- People quietly fix what systems disagree on. Agents can’t.
- Much of management is carrying decisions between people who can’t see the
  whole.

## What We Do

It sells by demonstration the reader completes, not by description. Each stage
of the cycle (See, Declare, Refactor, Prototype, Scale) carries:

1. **A premise** the buyer can check against their own organization.
2. **The move**: what the stage does, and what it earns.
3. **The next question** their own reasoning asks, set as the way on.

Scale is about the business, not the technology or the process design: the
organization doing the thing it exists to do and delivering its value.

The questions chain the stages into one argument. The last one becomes the
conversation: _Start with one stream_. Never describe a stage flatly; each one
must leave the reader wanting the next.

## Voice: quiet authority

- **Declare.** Plain statements about the world, as fact. No hedging, no hype.
- **Never describe TAL or the founder.** No adjectives about quality, rarity,
  or expertise. No "leading", "expert", "one of the few".
- **We and our.** TAL speaks as a company: a team that does the work, never
  one person.
- **Offense, not defense.** Name the capability that becomes possible, not the
  failure prevented. Avoid defining value by _never_, _cannot_, _without_.
- **No size or modesty words.** No _independent_, _small_, _boutique_.
- **No selling.** What We Do demonstrates the work; it is not a services
  list. No tiers, no per-product asks.

## Say the unsaid, with dignity

The buyer is an executive who has seen every operating model and change
program. Hype and threat both read as amateur. What lands is the plain truth
they already suspect:

- Some rules go unwritten because there was too much to write; some because no
  one wanted to say them. Name both, once, as mechanism.
- Making the organization explicit changes management. Say what coordination
  was and what leadership remains, with no villains and no flattery. The
  reader should recognize the work they already wished they were doing.
- State consequences plainly; never perform candor.

## Show, don't tell

Making the buyer feel seen is showing: name a particular they have lived and
would recognize (five answers to one question, a pilot no customer noticed),
then show the act that changes it. Benefit words and aphorisms about value are
telling, however well phrased.

- Proofs are reasoned from **first principles**: no case studies, no company
  names, no statistics, no history.
- **Never put the founder's résumé on the page**, and never dress it up as
  evidence. A powerful person doesn't list achievements.
- Never fill a slot by **copying existing text** (book lines, posts, repo
  descriptions). Synthesize what it means and say it newly.
- The founder is identified by a **declaration of how he thinks**, not by his
  record, his output, quotations, or praise:

  > **Kyle J. Tobin** founded TAL to build organizations the way they will now
  > have to run: whole, explicit, and executable, from the authority at the top
  > to the code underneath. He thinks in both directions at once.

## Less is more, quality over quantity

- Each element has one job; say each idea once.
- The headline argues, the proof shows, one line at most adds the rest.
- Choose the strongest few; leave out what is merely true.

## The works

They share a conviction, not a pipeline. Never present them as layers of one
system.

- **The Automated Organization** (book): the organization is the thing being
  transformed. Find the organization that actually operates; make its
  authority explicit enough to delegate; govern only what binds.
- **ONTOK** (open-source language): the organization's operational world model
  as code. A closed core every organization refines into its own kinds; a fact
  exists only if it is proven.
- **Type Construction Architecture** (open-source method): software correct by
  construction. A type is both the limit and the instruction, so agents build
  to it at full speed.

## Working rules learned the hard way

- Do the synthesis. Commit to a judgment; don't hide behind lists, quotes,
  records, or questions.
- Evolve the existing design; never wipe and restart.
- Make decisions inside the domain instead of handing them back.
- Copy is verified like layout: read it whole, aloud, as a stranger would.
