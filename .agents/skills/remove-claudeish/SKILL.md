# Remove Claudeish

Claudeish is prose that performs its own significance. The reader feels the writer landing sentences, staging contrasts, and announcing that something important is about to be said. Once a reader detects that, they discount what follows, including the parts that were earned.

The patterns are sentence shapes. A banned-word list misses them. Each shape is ordinary English and appears in good writing. The tell is how often it appears. One compressed line can carry a mechanism the reader has just been shown. A section full of them makes the piece sound generated, and the line that mattered disappears.

They pile up where revision rewards a sentence for sounding sharp on its own. Compression removes repeated content and leaves the most polished survivors. The passage revised most recently is where the density is highest, including lines someone praised.

Authority comes from what is specifically described: because this happens, that becomes necessary; that produces this result; here is what to do.

## The correction

Replace the performance with the mechanism, the evidence, or the instruction it stood in for. If none of those is in the sentence, delete it. If the paragraph gets weaker, the sentence was covering for a missing fact. Supply that fact in a plain sentence.

## Patterns

- **Negation-then-claim.** "X is not Y. It is Z." The negated thing was often never proposed. State the claim. Keep the negation when the reader holds that alternative, and say why it fails.
- **Aphoristic ender.** The paragraph closes on a shorter, neater sentence, built to be quoted. One in a piece can land. One per section is a cadence. End on the fact or the instruction. Delete an ender that only restates. Expand one that carries a new consequence until the consequence is visible.
- **Mirrored symmetry.** Two clauses on one frame with the slots swapped. The second clause is usually bent to fit. Write what happens, why, and what follows, and let the two halves stay as unequal as the facts.
- **Colon reveal and cleft.** The payload waits behind a colon, or a plain verb is restated as "what X did was." Put the fact in the main clause.
- **Section announcer.** "Here is the move." The sentence only says that content is coming. Delete it and start with the content.
- **Anaphora.** Three or more sentences in a row open on the same words. Two can compare. Three chant. Vary the third, or merge the series into one sentence.
- **Staccato run and fragment.** Short declaratives in a row, or a fragment used as a beat, after the causal links were cut. A short sentence is fine when the thought is short. Put back "because," "so," and "which." Turn a fragment into a sentence unless it quotes speech or gives an instruction.
- **Intensifier.** actually, exactly, really, genuinely, precisely, quietly, simply, merely. The word asserts a degree the sentence has not shown. Delete it. If the sentence weakens, add the specific it was pointing at.
- **Placement and agency metaphor.** An abstraction is given a place or a will: "lives in," "knows," "refuses," "the door," "the room." Say the plain fact. Keep the image only where the piece defined it and is using that definition.
- **Invented observation.** "Most teams…," "people routinely…," "anyone who has…" The authority is borrowed from a crowd the reader cannot check. State the mechanism.
- **Deflating tail.** "and nothing else," "which is none," "and no more," after the sentence already finished. Delete the tail.
- **Self-echo.** A concrete detail — a weekday, a duration, a percentage — comes back as rhythm after its first use as evidence. Keep it where it is the fact. Drop it where it adds nothing.
- **Rule of three.** A list of exactly three, or "the X, the Y, the Z," with the count chosen for the sound. Use the count the material has.
- **Significance signaling.** "That is the problem." "This is where it breaks." The sentence says that something matters. Delete it. If the importance is invisible without it, supply the mechanism.

## Keep

- **Hard claims.** Assert what the work has established. Leave open what it has not. Softening a demonstrated claim is a worse error than any pattern above.
- **A handful of compressions.** Short lines that name a mechanism already shown on the page. Choose them before editing anything else. They work because that shape is rare around them.
- **Parallelism that organizes.** A turn through each reader, or through the stations of a process, is a structure. Keep it.
- **A real contrast.** The negation stays when the alternative was actually on the table.
- **Specifics used as evidence.** Names, codes, counts, durations. Remove one only when it is being reused for rhythm.
- **Direct instruction.** An imperative that tells the reader what to do, including a short one in a procedure.
- **Defined terms, tables, and lists.** Audit the prose around an instrument. Leave the instrument, and leave a term the piece defined.

## The test

A flagged sentence carries a mechanism, a piece of evidence, an instruction, a real alternative, or a chosen compression — rewrite the shape and keep the content. It carries only cadence — delete it. Deleting it weakens the paragraph — supply what it was covering for.

## The pass

Run this on a finished piece, before delivery.

1. Reread the whole piece as the intended reader. Do not search for words. Mark every place you noticed the writer.
2. Name the family for each mark. One passage can belong to several.
3. Write down the compressions you are keeping. A handful. Every other line of that shape is now a candidate.
4. Correct paragraph by paragraph. Treat a run or a series as one unit. Apply the test.
5. Check each section. One landing line can stand. One per paragraph means the mechanism is still missing.
6. Reread for momentum. The hard claims are still hard, the kept lines stand out, and each paragraph still gives the reader something new. Where a passage has gone slack, put the content back in plain sentences.

The pass is done when the reader takes the authority from what is described.
